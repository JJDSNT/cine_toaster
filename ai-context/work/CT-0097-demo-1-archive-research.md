# What

**Paused (2026-10-16):** the user chose another first demo, the evolution
of animation (CT-0099). This research is kept as it is for a later demo.

The first demo (footage joined by ray-traced CG, roadmap "demo set") starts
from found material, not from a script (the user, 2026-10-16: "the
screenplay must be born from the material found, not the reverse"). An
inventory of usable sequences -- with timecodes and cinematographic notes --
is made first; synopses only after watching the best selects.

First round: four Chaplin shorts -- *The Pawnshop* (1916), *Behind the
Screen* (1916), *The Rink* (1916), *A Film Johnnie* (1914). Second round,
the inventory ready to receive them: Keaton and Méliès.

# Why

Finding the film in the material is itself what the demo shows: the tool lets
the creative process discover the film, rather than bending it to a
pre-defined technical showcase. It also exercises a capability the tool does
not have yet: archive research / footage selects.

# Decisions (the user's)

- **The work's rights and the copy's rights are kept apart.** Each copy
  records its origin, the specific file, the rights statement, restrictions
  and the evidence consulted. A downloadable copy is not therefore reusable.
- **The source is never altered.** Shot detection, stabilisation, cadence,
  restoration, contact sheets are traceable derivatives.
- **Narrative value and technical complexity are recorded apart.** A hard
  sequence may inspire the best story.
- **No restored material** (2026-10-16): a modern restoration carries its
  own rights, and the demo wants the material as it survived.
- **The Juggler's mirrored sphere is an option, not a requirement.**
- **Archive research is a capability found by the production**, not a
  subsystem built before the film: the first inventory is simple structured
  records and contact sheets; what deserves to become a feature is decided
  by the experience.
- The story is not chosen before the best selects are watched.
- **Modernisation is a narrative tool** (the user, 2026-10-16: "more
  elegant"): the archive starts as it survived and is modernised *within*
  the film, as the CG comes in -- not restored beforehand to match it, nor
  the CG aged to hide it. It fits "no restored material": the restoration
  is ours, on screen, as traceable derivatives (super-resolution, deflicker,
  stabilisation, cleaning), and the copy's quality still bounds what can be
  recovered. Pulls the restoration chain as a capability (the upscale spike,
  CT-0053).
- **Only the law filters** (2026-10-16): the work, the copy and the
  archive's terms must pass; everything else is noted, never a filter. A
  moving camera, hard occlusions, complex light are technical complexity,
  recorded apart from narrative value: chosen, they become the tool's work
  (the camera solve is in the roadmap; the material pulls it). The agent had
  proposed a fixed camera as a search criterion -- the material bent to the
  tool, against the policy of development pulled by productions; withdrawn.
  The copy's own limits (scan resolution, defects) are recorded as data of
  the copy: derivatives can treat them, not recover what is not there.
- The legal criterion proposed (diligence documented, not a lawyer's
  guarantee): public domain everywhere -- published before 1931 (US) and
  authors dead before 1956 (life + 70) -- or an explicit licence by the
  holder (CC0, CC BY, CC BY-SA); a faithful scan, no restoration, music,
  remade titles or colour; the archive's terms; people's image and
  trademarks. Sources by certainty: Library of Congress paper prints,
  Prelinger Archives, US National Archives, Open Images / Beeld en Geluid /
  EYE / Europeana, archive copies of authors dead before 1956 (Méliès,
  Lumière, Chomón, Porter). Open: the jurisdiction the demo answers to.

## Rights of the work (2026-10-16)

- The official Chaplin site: the films Chaplin made before 1918 for
  Keystone, Essanay and Mutual are in the public domain; image restorations
  and music added to a release may be protected by others
  (https://www.charliechaplin.com/en/infos/344-Material-for-Educational-Purpose).
- *City Lights* (1931, the blind flower girl; the user asked, 2026-10-16):
  under copyright. In the US, a 1931 work enters the public domain on
  2027-01-01 (95 years) -- to be verified against the Copyright Office's
  records; elsewhere Chaplin's life + 70 runs to the end of 2047. The Tramp's
  image is also claimed as a trademark. Not for a public demo now.
  (*Limelight* is 1952: protected far longer.)

## Safe sources, first look (2026-10-16)

- **Library of Congress** (paper prints): its site and API answer automated
  requests with a Cloudflare challenge or 403 -- browsed by a person, or
  through copies elsewhere that cite it.
- **Griffith Biograph on Commons**: almost every copy is from YouTube or
  Vimeo -- the copy's origin unknown.
- **Eye Filmmuseum on Commons** (102 files, uploaded from Open Images /
  openbeelden.nl): each copy is the archive's own transfer of its print,
  with its catalogue numbers (`EYE FLMnnnn`, `OB nnnnnn`) and a public
  domain statement -- the copy's origin documented by the holder of the
  print. 720x404 to 768x576. Among them **Bits & Pieces**: Eye's collection
  of unidentified fragments (a man spying on others, falling down the
  stairs, a love scene, two drunkards, a photo album, glass blowing,
  winter landscapes 1913, quick portrait artists c. 1900...) -- anonymous,
  out of their context, raw: material made for a new narrative. Also whole
  1910s-20s American and European shorts, and American Mutoscope & Biograph
  scenes (1899-1900). Each title's authors still to be checked against the
  life + 70 criterion; anonymous fragments run 70 years from publication.

## Round 1 under way (2026-10-16)

The production: `~/cine-toaster-demos/archive-hybrid/` (working title; the
story comes from the material). Its own tool, `tools/archive_research.py`
-- the production's, not Cine Toaster's yet (the capability found by the
production, ADR-free until the experience says what to keep):

- `research/archive/sources/<id>/original.<ext>`: the copy as the archive
  gives it, SHA-1 checked against the archive's, read-only; a download cut
  short is detected by its hash and fetched again.
- `sources/<id>/source.yaml`: the work (as the archive describes it, the
  licence templates it carries, a first status a person confirms), the
  copy (holder, catalogue numbers, file, hashes, size, intervention), the
  access terms, restrictions, evidence (the file page and the API's answer
  as consulted, with the time), status.
- `derived/<id>/shots.yaml` and `sheet-NN.jpg`: cuts found by FFmpeg's scene
  score (a derivative, never truth -- a flash or a splice may read as one),
  each shot's timecodes, contact sheets a shot a tile.
- `inventory.yaml`: the selects, narrative value and technical complexity
  apart.

Round 1: 43 Eye copies (1.4 GB) -- the Bits & Pieces fragments, the
Biograph scenes (1899-1900), Griffith (died 1948), Porter (1941), Cabanne
(1950), Ditmars (1942). Wikimedia answers 429 to a quick succession: the
tool waits as asked. Found on the way: Eye's pages say more than titles
(synopsis, tinting, genre, maker: BP113 is Pathé Frères, tinted brown) and
carry `PD-old-70` / `PD-anon-70-EU`.

## Round 1 watched (2026-10-16)

- 43 copies fetched whole (SHA-1 = the archive's), 492 shots found, 48
  contact sheets; every sheet seen except two of the longest films' later
  sheets. `research/archive/inventory.yaml`: 15 selects (each shot by
  number and timecode; narrative value and technical complexity apart;
  rights per select), 6 held back for rights or fitness, the rest
  described; `derived/selects-review-round-1.mp4` (9 min): the selects in
  motion, source and timecode burnt in.
- Strongest, by narrative value: *De Stad in Spiegelbeeld* (BP300, J.P.
  Smits, Amsterdam) -- a film of reflections ending on a **chrome sphere**
  reflecting the street: the Juggler's sphere is in the archive; a
  cameraman on a girder over Manhattan filming what we never see (BP352);
  Porter's *The Lighthouse by the Sea* (1911) -- a woman at a telescope, the
  sea in an iris; Griffith's *Edgar Allan Poe* (1909) -- a raven on the bust
  above the door; a glass mountain stormed by knights (BP125); a
  glassblower's glowing sphere (BP187); a portrait artist's film eaten by
  nitrate decay that clears within a minute (BP342: restoration as
  narrative, in the material).
- Found about the material: Eye's transfers run at 25 fps (the shooting
  speed was 16-18: motion sped up); 720x404 files hold a smaller 4:3
  picture (Poe: ~420 px wide); scene detection over-cuts decayed film and
  under-cuts dark tinted film, and merges a dissolve into one "shot" (BP300
  shot 7 holds the street and the sphere); several "anonymous" fragments
  carry distributors' titles (HAP-Film, Minerva, Pathé) or name people (Daisy
  Spiess, a 1920s German film): their makers are findable, so their rights
  are not settled by anonymity.

## Curation step 2 (2026-10-16)

At the user's request: the long films finished, the selects studied in
motion, feasibility by dimension, rights in three layers, three treatments.

- `tools/shot_study.py`: every frame of each select measured (camera drift
  and jolts by phase correlation, gate weave, flicker, hidden cuts) and a
  ten-frame filmstrip seen -- measured and sampled, not watched; noisy where
  large figures move, so each reading checked on its strip.
- `research/archive/curation-2.yaml`: corrections (BP300's sphere shot is two
  shots, and a hand touches the sphere; BP342 is a c. 1900 lightning-sketch
  trick film -- the drawing comes alive by substitution; the lighthouse's
  dilemma intertitle, "whom must I save?"; BP168 has no object between the
  hands), feasibility 1-5 by tracking / roto / depth / occlusion /
  reconstruction, rights as authorship / copy / reuse.
- Rights found: F. S. Armitage died 1933, George D. Baker (Bella Butts)
  died 1933 -- both past life + 70. Still open: BP300's "J.P. Smits" (Eye's
  database knows a Jan Smits, actor-producer 1926 -- not established as the
  same), BP125's film (not found: blocked). Next: ask Eye (Eye Film Sales).
- The archive's chrome sphere is a light probe: unwrapped, it gives the
  street's light of that day to light the CG.
- `research/treatments-round-1.md` (Portuguese, the film's language for the
  user): A *O operador* (the user's idea: a cameraman films the impossible;
  it appears in other archive films, reflection first, then the sphere,
  then out of the frame into the digital), B *Nevermore* (Griffith's raven
  answers the poet; his 1909 gesture becomes the reply), C *Luz sólida* (a
  glassblower's sphere becomes the lighthouse's lens).
- Speed (the user): the historical cadence stays; any change is an
  artistic decision per shot, recorded.
