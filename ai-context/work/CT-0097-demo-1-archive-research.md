# What

The first demo (footage joined by ray-traced CG, roadmap "demo set") starts
from found material, not from a script (the user, 2026-10-16: "the
screenplay must be born from the material found, not the reverse"). An
inventory of usable sequences -- with timecodes and cinematographic notes --
is made first; synopses only after watching the best selects.

First round: four Chaplin shorts -- *The Pawnshop* (1916), *Behind the
Screen* (1916), *The Rink* (1916), *A Film Johnnie* (1914). Second round,
the inventory ready to receive them: Keaton and Méliès.

# Why

Finding the film in the material is itself what the demo shows: the tool lets
the creative process discover the film, rather than bending it to a
pre-defined technical showcase. It also exercises a capability the tool does
not have yet: archive research / footage selects.

# Decisions (the user's)

- **The work's rights and the copy's rights are kept apart.** Each copy
  records its origin, the specific file, the rights statement, restrictions
  and the evidence consulted. A downloadable copy is not therefore reusable.
- **The source is never altered.** Shot detection, stabilisation, cadence,
  restoration, contact sheets are traceable derivatives.
- **Narrative value and technical complexity are recorded apart.** A hard
  sequence may inspire the best story.
- **No restored material** (2026-10-16): a modern restoration carries its
  own rights, and the demo wants the material as it survived.
- **The Juggler's mirrored sphere is an option, not a requirement.**
- **Archive research is a capability found by the production**, not a
  subsystem built before the film: the first inventory is simple structured
  records and contact sheets; what deserves to become a feature is decided
  by the experience.
- The story is not chosen before the best selects are watched.
- **Modernisation is a narrative tool** (the user, 2026-10-16: "more
  elegant"): the archive starts as it survived and is modernised *within*
  the film, as the CG comes in -- not restored beforehand to match it, nor
  the CG aged to hide it. It fits "no restored material": the restoration
  is ours, on screen, as traceable derivatives (super-resolution, deflicker,
  stabilisation, cleaning), and the copy's quality still bounds what can be
  recovered. Pulls the restoration chain as a capability (the upscale spike,
  CT-0053).
- **Only the law filters** (2026-10-16): the work, the copy and the
  archive's terms must pass; everything else is noted, never a filter. A
  moving camera, hard occlusions, complex light are technical complexity,
  recorded apart from narrative value: chosen, they become the tool's work
  (the camera solve is in the roadmap; the material pulls it). The agent had
  proposed a fixed camera as a search criterion -- the material bent to the
  tool, against the policy of development pulled by productions; withdrawn.
  The copy's own limits (scan resolution, defects) are recorded as data of
  the copy: derivatives can treat them, not recover what is not there.
- The legal criterion proposed (diligence documented, not a lawyer's
  guarantee): public domain everywhere -- published before 1931 (US) and
  authors dead before 1956 (life + 70) -- or an explicit licence by the
  holder (CC0, CC BY, CC BY-SA); a faithful scan, no restoration, music,
  remade titles or colour; the archive's terms; people's image and
  trademarks. Sources by certainty: Library of Congress paper prints,
  Prelinger Archives, US National Archives, Open Images / Beeld en Geluid /
  EYE / Europeana, archive copies of authors dead before 1956 (Méliès,
  Lumière, Chomón, Porter). Open: the jurisdiction the demo answers to.

# Done

## Rights of the work (2026-10-16)

- The official Chaplin site: the films Chaplin made before 1918 for
  Keystone, Essanay and Mutual are in the public domain; image restorations
  and music added to a release may be protected by others
  (https://www.charliechaplin.com/en/infos/344-Material-for-Educational-Purpose).
- *City Lights* (1931, the blind flower girl; the user asked, 2026-10-16):
  under copyright. In the US, a 1931 work enters the public domain on
  2027-01-01 (95 years) -- to be verified against the Copyright Office's
  records; elsewhere Chaplin's life + 70 runs to the end of 2047. The Tramp's
  image is also claimed as a trademark. Not for a public demo now.
  (*Limelight* is 1952: protected far longer.)

## A second survey, checked (2026-10-16)

A survey the user brought (six copies) checked against the sources:

- *A Trip to the Moon* (Méliès, 1902), Commons `Le Voyage dans la Lune
  (1902).webm`, 1440x1080: the **work** is public domain everywhere
  (`PD-old-auto-expired|1938`, Méliès died 1938); the **copy** has no source
  -- the page's Source field repeats the title. A 1440x1080 transfer of 1902
  is most likely a modern restoration's (Lobster/Flicker Alley); not cleared
  until its origin is found. The colour version (2011 restoration, Air's
  score) is out.
- *The Pawnshop* 1080p and *Behind the Screen* 1440x1072: the YTS Blu-ray
  rips found earlier -- out (restoration, illegitimate source).
- *One Week* (Keaton, 1920): Lobster's 2016 restoration -- out by the
  no-restoration rule; Keaton died 1966, so life + 70 runs to 2036.
- *A Film Johnnie* 1280x720 from YouTube, *The Rink* 400x300 (the `CC_`
  transfer, its non-free soundtrack removed): origin of the copy unknown.
- Chaplin (a British national, died 1977) under "distributable
  internationally": life + 70 to 2047 where that rule applies -- the risk
  the user's requirement excludes.
- What passes the international requirement by the work: authors dead
  before 1956 and published before 1931 -- Méliès, the Lumières, Porter,
  Chomón, and **D. W. Griffith's Biograph shorts (1908-1913; Griffith died
  1948)**, many of them in the Library of Congress paper prints: dramatic
  material, with a copy of known origin. To survey.
